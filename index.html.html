The Rising Cost of Home: A 25-Year Look at New York's Housing Market

By Kaden Stressler

In January of 2000, a middle-tier home in New York State was valued at approximately $150,000. By December 2024, that same tier of home carried an estimated price of around $479,000. Which according to the ZIllow Home Value Index is a 219 percent increase over 25 years. That figure is not just an outlier. Moreover it is a result of two distinct market shocks separated by more than a decade: run-up to the financial crisis, which peaked in 2008, and the rise during the pandemic, which started in 2020 and accelerated more quickly than nearly any other time in history. For many New Yorkers who aspire to be homeowners, those figures are more than just statistics. They stand for an entrance that has been gradually closing.

The ZHVI does not measure the most expensive or least expensive properties, but instead the estimated dollar value of a typical home in the center third of a state's market. It is based on Zillow's Zestimate methodology, which generates a smooth, adjusted for the season monthly estimate using listing data, property records, and standard statistical modeling. The cost of a typical home in that level increased by 38% in just four years, as seen by New York's index reading of $346,000 in January 2020 and $479,000 in December 2024. To put that in context, property values in New York rose more during those four pandemic years than they did for the course of the 2008–2018 decade. The data clearly shows the effects of the 2008 financial crisis. Early in 2008, New York home values peaked at about $280,000, but by 2012, they had dropped precipitously to about $248,000, a decrease of about 11% as the mortgage crisis deepened and defaults swept the market. It took a while to recover. New York did not achieve those pre-crisis levels for almost ten years. Everything changed when the pandemic struck.

According to Forrest Mas, a real estate manager with direct experience of the New York market, the information is exactly in line with what he saw during the pandemic. According to him, there was almost no inventory, and properties frequently sold for more than the asking price within days of being listed. Entry-level housing, or the modest single-family homes and condos that first-time purchasers rely on, was the market sector most impacted. Mas claims that in the majority of New York market segments, that niche has essentially vanished. "There's no such thing as a starter home anymore," he said. "Everyone is competing for the same units, and there just aren't enough affordable options in the city." That shortage has long-standing, systemic origins. The regulatory framework in New York makes building new homes costly and time-consuming. Building limitations in many municipalities restrict the quantity of new development, construction costs are among the highest in the country, and permitting processes are drawn out. As a result, demand continuously exceeds supply in the market, and each new buyer wave fights for essentially the same stock of properties that were available a generation ago.

Although New York's 38 percent increase during the epidemic is impressive, it is not as high as the states that led the country at that time. Between 2020 and 2024, bigger percentage improvements were reported by New Hampshire, Maine, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Georgia, and Idaho. Mas ascribed New York's somewhat slow expansion to the same structural elements that have historically characterized the state's market: high taxes, a lack of new building, and a development-discouraging regulatory framework. In contrast, Sun Belt states attracted a significant number of remote workers and retirees during the epidemic years because to their favorable business climates, lower tax costs, and a supply chain of new inventory. "New England is a hard place to live," Mas stated. "It's expensive, and those other markets had a lot more room to grow."

The states with the highest pre-pandemic prices were not always the ones that lead the country in the expansion of property values during the pandemic. Among states with complete data, New Hampshire saw a major percentage rise, closely followed by Maine, Connecticut, and Rhode Island. Demand was significantly higher than what local inventory might suffer as a result of these smaller Northeastern states accepting a wave of buyers priced out of Boston and New York City. Georgia, Florida, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arizona were amongst the Southern states that profited from a combination of in-migration, low tax regimes, and ongoing new construction development.

However, Mas emphasized that there might be a turning point in the Sun Belt's growth trajectory. The supply of new homes has significantly increased in a number of high-growth areas, particularly Florida, raising concerns about whether demand can support present price levels. "The question," he replied, "is whether there's too much product there right now." Values that increased significantly during the pandemic years may be under pressure to decline if there is an overstock in such markets.The issue in New York is the opposite. Even slight increases in demand result in dramatic price effects due to a chronic lack of housing supply, high building costs, and complicated permitting systems. According to further research, property values in New York will continue to rise and the number of people who can afford them will continue to decline until the foundational circumstances that limit new developments improve.

About the Data: The Zillow House Value Index (ZHVI) estimates average house value for middle-tier properties (33rd to 67th percentile) in a given region. In contrast to a simple median sale price, the ZHVI makes use of Zillow's Zestimate approach, which generates a smoothed, seasonally adjusted dollar estimate of the average home's value at a specific moment in time utilizing listing data, property records, and statistical modeling. Numerical dollars are used to report all values. Three states: Montana, North Dakota, and Wyoming were left out of the 2000–2024 percentage change estimate because they did not have enough data before 2004.
